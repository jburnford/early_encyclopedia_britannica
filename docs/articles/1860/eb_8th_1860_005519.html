4. In order to express \( X^o, Y^o, X', Y' \), the co-ordinates of the earth, which belong to the extreme observations, in a function of the time, and of the co-ordinates \( X, Y \) relative to the time \( t = 0 \), let us assume, as in § 1,

\[ V = 1 - \frac{\theta}{2R^3} - \frac{K \theta}{2R^5}, \quad U = -\delta + \frac{\theta}{6R^3} + \frac{K \theta}{4R^5} \] \[ V' = 1 - \frac{\theta}{2R^3} + \frac{K \theta}{2R^5}, \quad U' = \delta - \frac{\theta}{6R^3} + \frac{K \theta}{4R^5} \]

and we shall have

\[ X^o = VX + U \frac{dX}{dt}, \quad X' = V'X + U' \frac{dX}{dt} \]

the symbols \( V, U, R, K \), having now the same significance relatively to the orbit of the earth which \( v, u, r, k \), had before relatively to the orbit of the comet.

In consequence of these last equations, and of the equations (4), we have

\[ \lambda X' = -\frac{uV'}{u' - u} X - \frac{uU'}{u' - u} \frac{dX}{dt} \] \[ \lambda' X' = \frac{uV'}{u' - u} X + \frac{uU'}{u' - u} \frac{dX}{dt} \] \[ (1 - e) X = \frac{w}{u' - u} X \]

and substituting these values, in the first of equations (12),

\[ M = \frac{uV - uV' - w}{u' - u} X + \frac{uU - uU'}{u' - u} \frac{dX}{dt} \]

Substituting now for \( u, u', V, V' \), their values in terms of \( \theta \) and \( \theta' \), we obtain

\[ uV - uV' = \delta + \delta' - \frac{\theta(\theta + \theta')}{2R^3} \]

but

\[ u' - u = \delta + \delta' - \frac{\theta(\theta + \theta')}{6R^3} + \frac{k}{4R^5} (\theta' - \theta) \]

therefore, by dividing, and retaining only the terms which do not exceed the third order in respect of \( \theta \) and \( \theta' \),

\[ \frac{uV - uV'}{u' - u} = 1 - \frac{\theta(\theta + \theta')}{2R^3} + \frac{K \theta}{2R^5} (\theta' - \theta) \]

whence

\[ \frac{uV - uV' - w}{u' - u} = e - \frac{\theta(\theta + \theta')}{2R^3} + \frac{K \theta}{2R^5} (\theta' - \theta) \]

In like manner we have

\[ \frac{uU - uU'}{u' - u} = \frac{\theta(\theta - \theta')}{6} \left( \frac{1}{r^3} - \frac{1}{R^3} \right) \]

therefore, by division,

\[ \frac{uU - uU'}{u' - u} = \frac{\theta(\theta - \theta')}{6} \left( \frac{1}{r^3} - \frac{1}{R^3} \right) \]

The value of \( M \) therefore becomes

\[ M = \left( e - \frac{\theta(\theta - \theta')}{2R^3} + \frac{K \theta}{2R^5} (\theta' - \theta) \right) X \] \[ + \frac{\theta(\theta - \theta')}{6} \left( \frac{1}{r^3} - \frac{1}{R^3} \right) \frac{dX}{dt} \]

It will be remarked that the quantity

\[ \frac{\theta(\theta - \theta')}{2R^3} - \frac{K \theta}{2R^5} (\theta' - \theta) \]

is exactly analogous to that which has already been denoted by \( e \). Let us therefore suppose it to be represented by \( E \), and the last equation will become

\[ M = (e - E) X + \frac{\theta(\theta - \theta')}{6} \left( \frac{1}{r^3} - \frac{1}{R^3} \right) \frac{dX}{dt} \]

but on rejecting quantities involving higher powers than the third of \( \theta \) and \( \theta' \), we have obviously

\[ \frac{\theta - \theta'}{3} (e - E) = \frac{\theta(\theta - \theta')}{6} \left( \frac{1}{r^3} - \frac{1}{R^3} \right) \]

therefore ultimately

\[ M = (e - E) \left\{ X + \frac{\theta - \theta'}{3} \frac{dX}{dt} \right\} \]

Since the epoch or instant from which the time is reckoned is supposed to fall on the second observation, it is evident that \( \frac{\theta - \theta'}{3} \) is the instant corresponding to the mean time of the three observations. Let the value of \( X \) at this instant be denoted by \( X_1 \), and the corresponding values of \( R \) and \( A \) by \( R_1 \) and \( A_1 \), then

\[ M = (e - E) X_1 \]

By proceeding exactly in the same manner with regard to \( N \), we shall find

\[ N = (e - E) Y_1 \]

therefore, on substituting for \( X \) and \( Y \) their values in terms of \( R \) and \( A \), we have at the mean time of the three observations

\[ M = (e - E) R_1 \cos A_1 \] \[ N = (e - E) R_1 \sin A_1 \]

5. We now resume the equations (13). The unknown quantities which they contain are the three curvate distances \( \theta, \theta', \theta'' \), together with \( r \), the radius vector of the comet, which is involved in the values of \( \lambda, \lambda' \), and \( e \). They may be combined in various ways, and the different combinations will afford so many different expressions for \( \theta, \theta', \theta'' \), which will be more or less easily computed according to the circumstances of the case. The most obvious solu- Comet will be obtained by eliminating \( \varepsilon \), \( \xi \), and \( \zeta \). For this purpose the third of equations (13) gives

\[ \varepsilon = \frac{(1 - e) p^2 - \lambda q^2}{Nq^2} \]

and on substituting this value of \( \varepsilon \) in the first and second of these equations, they become respectively

\[ 0 = (m^2p^2 - m^2p') \lambda q^2 + (m^2p - mp^2)(1 - e) \xi + p^2M \]

\[ 0 = (m^2p^2 - m^2p') \lambda q^2 + (m^2p - mp^2)(1 - e) \xi + p^2N \]

On eliminating \( \lambda q^2 \) from the two equations now found, we get

\[ \varepsilon = \frac{(m^2p^2 - m^2p') M - (m^2p^2 - m^2p') N}{1 - e(m^2p^2 - m^2p')(m^2p - mp^2) - (m^2p^2 - m^2p')(m^2p - mp^2)} \]

an equation which becomes by reduction

\[ \varepsilon = \frac{1}{1 - e} \cdot \frac{(m^2N - m^2M)p^2}{(m^2p^2 - m^2p')(m^2p - mp^2) + (m^2p^2 - m^2p')(m^2p - mp^2)} \]

and on restoring the values of \( M, N, m, n, p, \) &c., given by equations (14) and (9), and recollecting that \( \sin(x - y) = \sin x \cos y - \cos x \sin y \), we get

\[ \varepsilon = \frac{e - E}{1 - e} \cdot R_1 \times \]

\[ \frac{\sin(A_1 - a^2) \tan b' - \sin(A_1 - a^2) \tan b'}{\sin(a^2 - a^2) \tan b' + \sin(a^2 - a^2) \tan b' + \sin(a^2 - a^2) \tan b'} \]

Let us assume

\[ P = \sin(A_1 - a^2) \tan b' - \sin(A_1 - a^2) \tan b' \]

\[ Q = \sin(a^2 - a^2) \tan b' + \sin(a^2 - a^2) \tan b' + \sin(a^2 - a^2) \tan b' \]

this assumption gives

\[ \varepsilon = \frac{P}{Q} \cdot \frac{e - E}{1 - e} \cdot R_1 \]

but on carrying the approximation only to quantities of the third order in respect of the time,

\[ \varepsilon = \frac{e - E}{1 - e} \cdot \frac{\theta}{2} \left( \frac{1}{r^3} - \frac{1}{R^3} \right) \]

therefore if we make \( \frac{\theta}{2} \frac{PR_1}{Q} = h \), the last equation will become

\[ \varepsilon = h \left( \frac{1}{r^3} - \frac{1}{R^3} \right) \]

6. The equation which has now been obtained contains two unknown quantities \( \varepsilon \) and \( r \); but another equation between the same variables may be readily found from the equations (10). These give, on restoring the values of \( m, n, p, X, \) and \( Y_1 \)

\[ x = \varepsilon \cos a + R \cos A, \]

\[ y = \varepsilon \sin a + R \sin A, \]

\[ z = \varepsilon \tan b, \]

whence, by squaring and adding

\[ x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = \varepsilon^2(1 + \tan^2b) + 2\varepsilon R \cos(A - a) + R^2; \]

but \( x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = r^2; \) and \( 1 + \tan^2b = \frac{1}{\cos^2b}; \)

therefore

\[ r^2 = R^2 + 2\varepsilon R \cos(A - a) + \frac{\varepsilon^2}{\cos^2b} \]

We have now arrived at two equations between \( r \) and \( \varepsilon \), by means of which the values of those quantities become known. In order to eliminate \( r \), let equation (16) be put under the form

\[ r^3(h + R^2) = hR^3, \]

the square of which is

\[ r^6(h + R^2)^2 = h^2R^6; \]

then, by substituting in this the value of \( r^6 \) given by equation (17) raised to the cube, there results

\[ \left\{ R^2 + 2\varepsilon R \cos(A - a) + \frac{\varepsilon^2}{\cos^2b} \right\}^3(h + R^2)^2 = h^2R^6 \]

an equation of the eighth degree in respect of the unknown quantity \( \varepsilon \). It is evident, however, that on being developed, \( h^2R^6 \) will disappear from both sides of it, after which the whole will be divisible by \( \varepsilon \); it belongs, therefore, in fact, only to the seventh degree. On being resolved by approximation it will give a value of \( \varepsilon \), but in practice it is more commodious to determine \( r \) and \( \varepsilon \) simultaneously from the equations (16) and (17), by the ordinary methods of false position.

7. Although the equations (16) and (17) thus appear to lead to a complete solution of the problem, it seldom happens in practice that an orbit can be determined in this manner. The reason of this is, that the quantity which we have represented by \( Q \) is always very small (it is not difficult to prove that it is of the third order in respect of \( \delta \)), and liable to be greatly affected by the errors of observation. If \( Q = 0 \), we have the equation

\[ \sin(A_1 - a^2) \tan b' + \sin(a^2 - a^2) \tan b' + \sin(a^2 - a^2) \tan b' = 0, \]

which denotes that the three apparent places of the comet are situated in the same great circle of the sphere. The value of \( Q \) therefore depends solely on the deviation of the comet's apparent path from a great circle of the heavens, during the short interval between the extreme observations; and this deviation is in general so small, that a slight error of observation, which it is impossible to guard against, may cause it to become evanescent, or even to change its sign. On this account the equation (16), which is called by Legendre the doubtful equation, can only be employed in very favourable circumstances; and it becomes necessary, in the greater number of cases, to have recourse to other methods of solution. Some conclusions may, however, be deduced from it, which will materially assist in guiding the subsequent calculations.

In the first place, as the curtaise distance \( \varepsilon \) is necessarily a finite quantity, if the denominator of the fraction expressed by \( h \) vanishes, that is, if \( Q = 0 \), the numerator must vanish at the same time. Now this can happen only in two ways: either by supposing \( P = 0 \), or \( \frac{1}{r^3} = \frac{1}{R^3} \).

If we suppose \( P = 0 \), we have the equation

\[ \sin(A_1 - a^2) \tan b' - \sin(A_1 - a^2) \tan b' = 0, \]

which indicates that the place of the sun at the instant

\[ \frac{\varepsilon}{3}, \]

corresponding to the mean time of the three observations, is situated in the same great circle of the sphere with the three apparent places of the comet. Now it is easy to see, that although the sun may not be exactly in the same great circle with the three apparent places of the comet, yet if it is near one of the intersections of that circle with the ecliptic, the quantity \( P \) will be very small, and the equation (16) in consequence will not lead to any satisfactory result.

In the second place, if \( Q \) vanishes while the value of \( P \) continues so great that its sign would not be changed by supposing its component parts to vary to the extent of the errors of observation, we must then have \( \frac{1}{r^3} = \frac{1}{R^3} \) or \( r = R \); that is to say, the comet and the earth are at the same distance from the sun. This circumstance, therefore, also renders equation (16) indeterminate. It may be remarked, however, that in this case we obtain an easy approximation to the value of \( \varepsilon \) from equation (17), which, when \( r \) is equal to \( R \), becomes

\[ \varepsilon = 2R \cos^2 b \cos(180^\circ + A - a), \] where $180 + \lambda$ is the longitude of the sun at the instant of the middle observation.

In the third place, when the quantities $P$ and $Q$ are both so great that their signs can be determined without ambiguity, equation (16) will show whether the distance of the comet from the sun is greater or less than the radius vector of the earth. For since $\varepsilon$ is necessarily positive, the sign of $\frac{P}{Q}$ must be the same as that of $e - E$ or

$$\frac{1}{r} = \frac{1}{R};$$ consequently, the sign of $\frac{P}{Q}$ being known, we also know whether $r$ is greater or less than $R$.

8. Since the state of the observations will seldom permit equation (16) to be employed with advantage, it becomes necessary to attempt some other combination of the three fundamental equations (15). Instead of eliminating the three curtate distances $\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon'$, $\varepsilon''$ from these equations, we may determine two of them, for example $\varepsilon$ and $\varepsilon'$, in terms of the third, $\varepsilon''$; by which means we shall obtain at least the ratios of these distances to one another. This may be effected in various ways; but we shall confine ourselves to the two following combinations, the one or other of which will be applicable in every case.

Let the first of the equations (13) be multiplied by $N$, and the second by $M$; the difference of the products is

$$\lambda \varepsilon' (N \varepsilon' - M \varepsilon') + \lambda \varepsilon'' (N \varepsilon'' - M \varepsilon'') - (1 - e) \varepsilon (N \varepsilon - M \varepsilon) = 0.$$ Combining this with the third equation, so as to eliminate first $\varepsilon'$ and then $\varepsilon''$, we get

$$\varepsilon = \frac{(1 - e)}{\lambda} \left( \frac{N \varepsilon' - M \varepsilon'}{N \varepsilon'' - M \varepsilon''} \right) p' - \frac{(N \varepsilon' - M \varepsilon')}{N \varepsilon'' - M \varepsilon''} p,$$

and, on substituting the values of $M$, $N$, $m$, &c. before found,

$$\varepsilon = \frac{(1 - e)}{\lambda} \left( \frac{N \varepsilon' - M \varepsilon'}{N \varepsilon'' - M \varepsilon''} \right) p' - \frac{(N \varepsilon' - M \varepsilon')}{N \varepsilon'' - M \varepsilon''} p.$$ These two equations will give the ratios $\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon'}$, $\frac{\varepsilon'}{\varepsilon''}$, in all cases when the common denominator (the quantity we have already denoted by $P$) is not so small as to render the expressions indeterminate. In general they will be most applicable when the motion of the comet in latitude is greater than its motion in longitude.

The other combination to which we have alluded, is formed by employing only the two first of the equations (13). Let the first be multiplied by $n'$, and the second by $m'$; the difference of the products gives

$$\varepsilon = \frac{(1 - e)}{\lambda} \left( \frac{m' \varepsilon' - m \varepsilon'}{m' \varepsilon'' - m \varepsilon''} \right) N \varepsilon' - M \varepsilon'.$$

In like manner, if we multiply the first by $n'$, and the second by $m'$, in order to eliminate $\varepsilon'$, we find

$$\varepsilon = \frac{(1 - e)}{\lambda} \left( \frac{m' \varepsilon' - m \varepsilon'}{m' \varepsilon'' - m \varepsilon''} \right) N \varepsilon' + M \varepsilon'.$$

On substituting the values of $M$, $N$, $m$, $n$, &c. in these last equations, they become

$$\varepsilon = \frac{(1 - e)}{\lambda} \left( \frac{m' \varepsilon' - m \varepsilon'}{m' \varepsilon'' - m \varepsilon''} \right) N \varepsilon' - M \varepsilon',$$

$$\varepsilon = \frac{(1 - e)}{\lambda} \left( \frac{m' \varepsilon' - m \varepsilon'}{m' \varepsilon'' - m \varepsilon''} \right) N \varepsilon' + M \varepsilon'.$$

The equations now found will be most applicable when the motion of the comet in longitude is greater than its motion in latitude.

It may be proper to remark, that on employing the equations (18) or (19) to determine the ratio of the curtate distances, we may suppose in the first trials $e = 0$, $E = 0$, $\lambda = \frac{\theta}{\delta + \theta}$, $\lambda' = \frac{\theta}{\delta + \theta}$. On this supposition the equations (19) become simply

$$\varepsilon = \frac{\theta}{\delta + \theta} \sin (\alpha - \alpha'),$$

$$\varepsilon' = \frac{\theta}{\delta + \theta} \sin (\alpha - \alpha').$$

11. From what has now been shown it may be inferred that the equations (13) can in general be used only in determining the ratios of $\varepsilon$ and $\varepsilon'$ to $\varepsilon$; on account of the smallness and consequent uncertainty of the denominator $Q$, they leave the absolute value of $\varepsilon$ indeterminate. It is therefore necessary to seek some other equation among the variables employed, before we can obtain a complete solution of the problem. For this purpose we must have recourse to some of the properties connected with the comet's motion in its orbit. No hypothesis has as yet been made respecting the nature of the orbit. If we suppose it to be a parabola, the laws of parabolic motion will give a new equation, without introducing any other variable than those which have already been employed, which may either be combined with equation (16), for the purpose of determining $r$ and $\varepsilon$ directly, or which may be employed to correct the results obtained by assigning a hypothetical value to $\varepsilon$ in the equations (18) or (19). There are various methods of introducing the condition derived from the nature of the parabolic motion; but, in the method of solution which we here follow, it is most convenient to employ the theorem of Lambert, which gives the time of describing a parabolic arc in terms of its chord, and the two radii vectors drawn to its extremities. Before demonstrating this theorem, however, it will be proper to show how the chord may be computed.

Let $c$ be the chord which joins $C^o C$. The co-ordinates of its extremities being $x'y', x''y''$, we have

$$c^2 = (x'^2 + y'^2) + (x''^2 + y''^2) + (x'^2 + y'^2)$$

$$= x'^2 + y'^2 + x''^2 + y''^2 + x'^2 + y'^2$$

$$= 2x'^2 + 2y'^2 - 2x''^2 - 2y''^2,$$

that is,

$$c^2 = r'^2 + r''^2 - 2(r'^2 + r''^2).$$

But by reason of the equations (8) and (10),

$$x'^2 = \varepsilon^2 \cos^2 \alpha + \varepsilon^2 R^2 \cos \alpha' + \varepsilon^2 R^2 \cos \alpha'' + \varepsilon^2 R^2 \cos \alpha'',$$

$$y'^2 = \varepsilon^2 \sin^2 \alpha + \varepsilon^2 R^2 \sin \alpha' + \varepsilon^2 R^2 \sin \alpha'' + \varepsilon^2 R^2 \sin \alpha'',$$

therefore, by adding and substituting for $m$, $n$, $p$, &c. their values given in equations (9)

$$x'^2 + y'^2 + x''^2 + y''^2 = \varepsilon^2 \cos (\alpha - \alpha') + \varepsilon^2 R^2 \cos (\alpha' - \alpha'')$$

$$+ \varepsilon^2 R^2 \cos (\alpha'' - \alpha') + \varepsilon^2 R^2 \cos (\alpha' - \alpha'').$$

hence, denoting the right hand side of this equation by $F$,

$$c^2 = r'^2 + r''^2 - 2F.$$

Comparing this last equation with

$$c^2 = r'^2 + r''^2 - 2r'^2 \cos \phi,$$

which is given by the triangle $C^o S^o C'$, we have $F = r'^2 \cos \phi$, or cos $\phi = \frac{F}{r'^2}$, $\phi$ being the angle included between the two radii vectors $r'$ and $r''$.

12. We now proceed with the demonstration of Lambert's theorem.

According to the doctrine of central forces, when a Comet. body describes an orbit in virtue of a force varying inversely as the square of the distance, the square of the velocity at the distance \( r \) given by the equation

\[ \frac{1}{r} - \frac{1}{a} = \frac{d^2}{dt^2} + \frac{dy^2}{dt^2}, \]

in which \( a \) is the major axis of the orbit, and the sum of the masses is supposed equal to unit. In the case of the parabola, the major axis is infinite, and the equation becomes simply

\[ \frac{1}{r} = \frac{d^2}{dt^2} + \frac{dy^2}{dt^2}. \]

Let \( D \) be the perihelion distance SP, and \( \psi \) the true anomaly, or the angle PSC. We have then \( x = r \cos \psi \), \( y = r \sin \psi \), whence \( \frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{dr}{dt} + r \frac{d\psi}{dt} \), and consequently

\[ \frac{2}{r} = \frac{d^2}{dt^2} + r^2 \frac{d\psi^2}{dt^2}. \]

But from the property of the parabola \( r = 2D - r \cos \psi \); hence \( r (1 + \cos \psi) = 2D \), or \( r \cos^2 \psi = D \), therefore

\[ r = D (1 + \tan^2 \frac{1}{2} \psi). \]

Let \( \tan \frac{1}{2} \psi = u \); then \( dr = 2Ddu \); and \( du = \frac{1}{2} d\psi \cos^2 \frac{1}{2} \psi \)

\[ = \frac{1}{2} d\psi (1 + \tan^2 \frac{1}{2} \psi), \]

whence \( d\psi = \frac{2du}{1 + u^2} \). Substituting these expressions in the equation \( \frac{2}{r} = \frac{d^2}{dt^2} + r^2 \frac{d\psi^2}{dt^2} \), it becomes \( d^2 = 2D^3 (1 + u^2)^2 du \), whence \( dt = \sqrt{2D^3 (1 + u^2)} du \); which, on being integrated, becomes

\[ t = \sqrt{2D^3} (u + \frac{1}{3} u^3). \]

Let \( u_0 \), \( u_0' \) be the values of \( u \) in this equation at the times \( t + \delta \), \( t - \delta \) respectively; we have then

\[ t + \delta = \sqrt{2D^3} (u_0 + \frac{1}{3} u_0^3), \]

\[ t - \delta = \sqrt{2D^3} (u_0' + \frac{1}{3} u_0'^3), \]

and, by subtracting,

\[ \delta + \delta' = \sqrt{2D^3} \left( u_0 - u_0' + \frac{1}{3} (u_0^3 - u_0'^3) \right). \]

In order to transform this expression into another containing only \( r^2 \) and \( c \), let \( \phi \) be the anomaly corresponding to \( C \), \( \psi \) that corresponding to \( C' \), \( \phi = \psi - \psi_0 = \text{angle } C^0SC' \), and \( \tan \frac{1}{2} \phi = v \). We have then \( \psi = \psi_0 + \phi \); and consequently \( \tan \frac{1}{2} \psi + \tan \frac{1}{2} \phi = \tan \frac{1}{2} (\psi_0 + \phi) = \tan \frac{1}{2} \psi_0 + \tan \frac{1}{2} \phi \), that is, \( v = \frac{u_0 + v}{1 - u_0 v} \). Now, if we put

\[ Z = u_0 - u_0' + \frac{1}{3} (u_0^3 - u_0'^3), \]

we shall have evidently

\[ Z = (u_0 - u_0') \left( 1 + \frac{1}{3} (u_0^2 + u_0'^2 + u_0 u_0') \right), \]

therefore, substituting \( \frac{v^2 + v}{1 - u_0 v} \) for \( u_0 \),

\[ Z = \frac{v (1 + u_0^2)}{1 - u_0 v} \left( 1 + \frac{1}{3} \frac{v^2 (1 + u_0^2)}{1 - u_0 v} \right); \]

that is,

\[ Z = \frac{v (1 + u_0^2)}{1 - u_0 v} \left( 1 + \frac{1}{3} \frac{v^2 (1 + u_0^2)}{1 - u_0 v} \right); \]

hence the formula for the time becomes

\[ \delta + \delta' = \sqrt{2D^3} \frac{v (1 + u_0^2)}{1 - u_0 v} \left( 1 + \frac{1}{3} \frac{v^2 (1 + u_0^2)}{1 - u_0 v} \right). \]

Again, the triangle \( C^0SC' \) gives the equation

\[ c^2 = r^2 + r'^2 - 2rr' \cos \phi, \]

but \( \tan \frac{1}{2} \phi = v \); therefore, from the trigonometrical formulae, \( \cos \phi = \frac{1 - v^2}{1 + v^2} \), whence, substituting and reducing,

\[ (r + r')^2 - c^2 = \frac{4v^2}{1 + v^2}. \]

For the sake of abridging, we shall put

\[ r + r' + c = 2s, \quad r + r' - c = 2d, \]

whence \( r + r' = s + d, \quad c = s - d, \quad \text{and} \quad (r + r')^2 - c^2 = 4sd \).

Comparing these with the above equation, we have

\[ \frac{v^2}{1 + v^2} = sd, \quad \text{whence} \quad v = \frac{(r + r')^2 - c^2}{(1 + v^2)^{\frac{1}{2}}} = \frac{(sd)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{(1 + v^2)^{\frac{1}{2}}}. \]

Now the polar equation to the parabola gives

\[ r = D (1 + u^2), \quad r' = D (1 + u'^2) = D \left( 1 + \frac{1}{u'^2} \right), \]

therefore \( sd = \frac{r^2}{1 + u^2} = D^2 \left( 1 + \frac{1}{u'^2} \right)^2 \), and consequently,

\[ \frac{1 + u^2}{1 - u^2 v} = \frac{(sd)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{D}. \]

From the same values of \( r^2 \) and \( r' \) we get

\[ r + r' = D \left( 1 + \frac{1}{u'^2} \right) \left\{ 1 + \frac{1}{(1 - u^2 v)^{\frac{1}{2}}} \right\} \]

\[ = \frac{2D (1 + u'^2)}{1 - u^2 v} + \frac{Dv^2 (1 + u'^2)^2}{(1 - u^2 v)^{\frac{3}{2}}}, \]

but \( r + r' = s + d, \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{1 + u^2}{1 - u^2 v} = \frac{\sqrt{sd}}{D}, \quad \text{therefore} \quad s + d = 2\sqrt{sd} + \frac{v^2 sd}{D}, \quad \text{whence} \quad v = \sqrt{D} \frac{\sqrt{s} - \sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{sd}}. \]

Substituting these values of \( v \) and \( \frac{1 + u^2}{1 - u^2 v} \) in the equation for the time, it becomes

\[ \delta + \delta' = \sqrt{2\sqrt{sd}} (\sqrt{s} - \sqrt{d}) \left\{ 1 + \frac{1}{3} \frac{(\sqrt{s} - \sqrt{d})^2}{\sqrt{sd}} \right\}, \]

that is

\[ \delta + \delta' = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{3} (\sqrt{s} - \sqrt{d}) (\sqrt{s} + \sqrt{d} + d), \]

or

\[ \delta + \delta' = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{3} (\sqrt{s} - \sqrt{d}) (\sqrt{s} - \sqrt{d} - d); \]

and, finally, on restoring the values of \( s \) and \( d \),

\[ \delta + \delta' = \frac{1}{2} (r + r' + c) - \frac{1}{2} (r + r' - c). \]

This is the very elegant theorem found by Euler, but commonly attributed to Lambert, by means of which we are enabled to correct the hypothetical values successively given to \( \phi \), till a value of that quantity is found which will satisfy all the equations as nearly as the observations will admit.

For this purpose it is usual to employ a table constructed from the following property of bodies moving in a parabolic orbit, namely, if \( T \) and \( T' \) be the times of describing the same anomaly in two parabolas whose perihelion distances are respectively \( D \) and \( D' \),

then \( T : T' :: D^{\frac{3}{2}} : D'^{\frac{3}{2}} \). Hence, if a table is formed showing the time \( T' \) in which every degree, &c., is described in a parabola whose perihelion distance is \( D' = 1 \), it will give the time \( T' \) in which the same anomaly is described in the parabola whose perihelion distance is \( D \), by simply multiplying the tabular time by \( D^{\frac{3}{2}} \); for when \( D' = 1 \), we have \( T = D^{\frac{3}{2}} T' \). The time \( T \) must be added to the epoch of the first observation, if the comet is approaching the perihelion of its orbit, but subtracted from that epoch if it has already passed the perihelion.

But instead of employing a table of parabolic motion, the time may be computed directly from the formula

\[ T = \sqrt{2} D^{\frac{3}{2}} (\tan \frac{1}{2} \varphi + \frac{1}{2} \tan^2 \frac{1}{2} \varphi), \]

observing to add to the logarithm of \( T \) the constant logarithm 1-7644179 (the compliment of 8-23555821), in order that \( T \) may be expressed in mean solar days.

To determine the position of the orbit, we must first compute the heliocentric latitudes and longitudes. Let \( \alpha^o \) and \( \alpha' \) be the heliocentric longitudes of the points \( C^o \) and \( C' \); \( \beta^o \) and \( \beta' \) their heliocentric latitudes; then, denoting for an instant by \( \sigma \) the comet's distance from the earth, we shall have \( r \sin \beta = \sigma \sin b \); but \( \sigma = \varepsilon \sec b \), therefore

\[ \sin \beta = \frac{\varepsilon}{r} \tan b, \quad \sin \beta' = \frac{\varepsilon}{r'} \tan b. \]

These two formulae, which are very easily computed, give the heliocentric latitudes at the extreme observations. In the present case they become

\[ \sin \beta^o = (9-4155979), \quad \sin \beta' = (9-4341627). \]

Now, to compute the heliocentric longitudes, let us suppose \( K \) to be the projection of \( C \) on the plane of the ecliptic. In the rectilinear triangle \( ESK \), formed by joining the positions of the earth, the sun, and the projected place of the comet, we have \( KE = \varepsilon \); \( SK = r \cos \beta \); the angle \( ESK \), opposite to \( KE \), \( = a - A \); the difference between the heliocentric longitude of the comet and the longitude of the earth; and the angle \( KES \), opposite to \( SK \), \( = a - A \); the difference between the geocentric longitude of the comet and the longitude of the sun; therefore

\[ \varepsilon : r \cos \beta :: \sin(a - A) : \sin(a - A), \]

from which, since \( \frac{\varepsilon}{r} = \tan \beta \), we deduce for the first and third observations,

\[ \sin(a^o - A^o) = \tan \beta^o \sin(a^o - A^o), \]

\[ \sin(a' - A') = \tan \beta' \sin(a' - A'). \]

Let \( N \) be the ascending node, \( \gamma \) its longitude, and \( I \) the inclination of the orbit. In the spherical triangle \( NCK \), the side \( NK = a - \gamma \); \( CK = \beta \), and the angle \( CNK = I \).

Therefore, by Napier's analogies, \( \cot I = \cot \beta \sin(a - \gamma) \), which, on substituting the longitudes \( a^o \) and \( a' \), gives the double equation

\[ \cot I = \cot \beta \sin(a^o - \gamma) = \cot \beta \sin(a' - \gamma); \]

from which we have

\[ \frac{\sin(a^o - \gamma)}{\sin(a' - \gamma)} = \frac{\tan \beta^o}{\tan \beta'}, \]

whence

\[ \sin(a^o - \gamma) + \sin(a' - \gamma) = \tan \beta^o + \tan \beta', \]

\[ \sin(a^o - \gamma) - \sin(a' - \gamma) = \tan \beta^o - \tan \beta'. \]

But if \( A \) and \( B \) represent two arcs, then, by the trigonometrical formulae,

\[ \frac{\sin A + \sin B}{\sin A - \sin B} = \frac{\tan \frac{1}{2}(A + B)}{\tan \frac{1}{2}(A - B)} \]

and

\[ \frac{\tan A + \tan B}{\tan A - \tan B} = \frac{\sin(A + B)}{\sin(A - B)}; \]

whence the above equation becomes

\[ \frac{\tan \left( \frac{1}{2}(a^o + a') - \gamma \right)}{\tan \left( \frac{1}{2}(a^o - a') \right)} = \frac{\sin(\beta^o + \beta')}{\sin(\beta^o - \beta')}, \]

consequently

\[ \tan \left( \frac{1}{2}(a^o + a') - \gamma \right) = \frac{\sin(\beta^o + \beta')}{\sin(\beta^o - \beta')} \tan \frac{a^o - a'}{2}. \] The tangent now found belongs to two different angles; but this circumstance can occasion no ambiguity; for as the inclination I is supposed to be always less than 90°, the cotangent of I must be always positive, so that sin. The orbit determined according to the method which has here been explained, will always satisfy the first and third observations. In general it will also represent the second; but should it occur that the place of the comet at the second observation, computed from the elements thus found, differs considerably from its observed place, we may then conclude that the true orbit cannot be represented by a parabola. In this case recourse must be had to the ellipse, for computing which, the best method is that which is given by Gauss in his Theoria Motuum Corporum Coelestium.
