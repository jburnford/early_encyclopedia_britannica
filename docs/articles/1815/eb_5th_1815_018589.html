SESTERCE

Volume 19 · 746 words · 1815 Edition
← SESSION · Index · SESTOS →

Sesterius, a silver coin, in use among the ancient Romans, called also simply nummus, and sometimes nummus sesterius. The sesterius was the fourth part of the denarius, and originally contained two asses and a half. It was at first denoted by LLS; the two L's signifying two librae, and the S half. But the librarii, afterwards converting the two L's into an H, expressed the sesterius by HS. The word sesterius was first introduced by way of abbreviation for semisesterius, which signifies two, and a half of a third, or, literally, only half a third; for in expressing half a third, it was understood that there were two before.

Some authors make two kinds of sesterces; the less called sesterius, in the masculine gender; and the great one, called sesterium, in the neuter: the first, that we have already described; the latter containing a thousand of the other. Others will have any such distinction of great and little sesterces unknown to the Romans: sesterterius, say they, was an adjective, and signified, as sesterterius, or two asses and a half; and when used in the plural, as in quinquaginta sesterterii, or sesterteria, it was only by way of abbreviation, and there was always understood centena, millia, &c.

This matter has been accurately stated by Mr Raper, in the following manner. The substantive to which sesterterius referred is either as, or pondus; and sesterterius as is two asses and a half; sesterterium pondus, two pondera and a half, or two hundred and fifty denarii. When the denarius passed for ten asses, the sesterterius of two asses and a half was a quarter of it; and the Romans continued to keep their accounts in these sesterces long after the denarius passed for sixteen asses; till, growing rich, they found it more convenient to reckon by quarters of the denarius, which they called nummi, and used the words nummus and sesterterius indifferently, as synonymous terms, and sometimes both together, as sesterterius nummus; in which case the word sesterterius, having lost its original signification, was used as a substantive; for sesterterius nummus was not two nummi and a half, but a single nummus of four asses. They called any sum under two thousand sesterces so many sesterterii in the masculine gender; two thousand sesterces they called duo or bina sesterteria, in the neuter; so many quarters making five hundred denarii, which was twice the sesterterium; and they said dena, vicena, &c. sesterteria, till the sum amounted to a thousand sesterteria, which was a million of sesterces. But, to avoid ambiguity, they did not use the neuter sesterterium in the singular number, when the whole sum amounted to no more than a thousand sesterces, or one sesterterium. They called a million of sesterces decies nummorum, or decies sesterteriorum, for decies centena millia nummorum, or sesterteriorum (in the masculine gender), omitting centena millia for the sake of brevity. They likewise called the same sum decies sesterterium (in the neuter gender) for decies centes sesterterium, omitting centes for the same reason; or simply decies, omitting centena millia sesterterium, or centes sesterterium; and with the numeral adverbs decies, vicies, centes, millies, and the like, either centena millia or centes was always understood. These were their most usual forms of expression; though for bina, dena, vicena sesterteria, they frequently said bina, dena, vicena millia nummorum. If the consular denarius contained 60 troy grains of fine silver, it was worth something more than eight-pence farthing and a half sterling; and the as, of 16 to the denarius, a little more than a half-penny. To reduce the ancient sesterces of two asses and a half, when the denarius passed for 16, to pounds sterling, multiply the given number by 5454, and cut off six figures on the right hand for decimals. To reduce nummi sesterterii, or quarters of the denarius, to pounds sterling; if the given sum be consular money, multiply it by 8727, and cut off six figures on the right hand for decimals; but for imperial money diminish the said product by one-eighth of itself. Phil. Tranf. vol. lxi. part ii. art. 48.

To be qualified for a Roman knight, an estate of 400,000 sesterces was required; and for a senator, of 800,000.

Authors also mention a copper sesterce, worth about one-third of a penny English.
