BANGUE

Volume 3 · 373 words · 1823 Edition
← BANGOR · Index · BANIALUCH →

a species of opiate, in great use throughout the east, for drowning cares and inspiring joy.—This by the Persians is called bengi; by the Arabs, essrar, corruptly asseral, and assarth; by the Turks, bengtii, and vulgarly called mastock; by the European naturalists, bangue or hang.—It is the leaf of a kind of wild hemp, growing in the countries of the Levant; it differs little, either as to leaf or seed, from our hemp, except in size. Some have mistaken it for a species of althea.

There are divers manners of preparing it, in different countries. Olearius describes the method used in Persia. Mr Sale tells us, that, among the Arabs, the leaf is made into pills, or conserves. But the most distinct account is that given by Alexander Maurocordato, counsellor and physician of the Ottoman Porte, in a letter to Wedelius. According to this author, hangue is made of the leaves of wild hemp, dried in the shade, then ground to powder; put into a pot wherein butter has been kept; set in an oven till it begin to terrify; then taken out, and pulverized again; thus to be used occasionally, as much at a time as will lie on the point of a knife. Such is the Turkish hangue.—The effects of this drug are, To confound the understanding; set the imagination loose; induce a kind of folly and forgetfulness, wherein all cares are lost, and joy and gaiety take place thereof. Bangue, in reality, is a succedaneum to wine, and obtains in those countries where Mahometanism is established; which prohibiting the use of that liquor absolutely, the poor Mussulmans are forced to have recourse to succedanea, to rouse their spirits. The principal are opium and this hangue. As to the opinion among Europeans, that the Turks prepare themselves for battle by a dose of hangue, which rouses their courage, and drives them, with eagerness, to certain death; Dr Maurocordato assures us, that it is a popular error: the Turks think they are then going assuredly to receive the crown of martyrdom; and would not, for any consideration, lose the merit of it, which they would do, by eating the hangue, as being held unlawful by their apostle, among other things which intoxicate.
