# Timber, Rot, and the Royal Navy: Tracing a Crisis Through the Encyclopedia Britannica (1771-1860)

*An analysis of how the Encyclopedia Britannica tracked the evolving science and political economy of timber, wood decay, and naval shipbuilding across eight editions published over ninety years.*

---

## 1. Introduction

The timber crisis that gripped Britain from the late eighteenth century through the mid-nineteenth century was one of the defining resource anxieties of the age. Oak forests were being depleted, the Royal Navy's ships were rotting faster than they could be replaced, and the science of why wood decayed was still poorly understood. The Encyclopedia Britannica, as Britain's premier general knowledge reference, tracked these developments in real time. This analysis traces the changing discourse across all eight editions (1771-1860), drawing on 331 topical articles and 1,475 keyword-extracted passages identified through systematic search.

**Data sources**: All quotations are drawn from the parsed OCR text of the National Library of Scotland's digitized Encyclopedia Britannica. Links point to the project site where full articles can be read.

In the 1st edition (1771), TIMBER merited only eight words: *"includes all kinds of felled and seasoned woods."* By the 8th edition (1860), it had become a 19,259-word treatise covering tree physiology, soil science, every major timber species, global supply chains, and detailed preservation chemistry. This 2,400-fold expansion reflects timber's transformation from a taken-for-granted material to an object of intense scientific, strategic, and economic concern.

The most dramatic single intervention is the **DRY-ROT** article, appearing only in the 1823 supplement to the 6th edition — 8,983 words of crisis literature, written in the immediate aftermath of the Queen Charlotte scandal.

## 3. The Dry Rot Crisis

### 3.1 The Queen Charlotte (1810)

The 1823 [DRY-ROT](docs/1823/vol501.html#article-DRY_ROT) article opens by calling dry rot *"a most destructive, and apparently infectious, disease in timber, which, by decomposing the fibres, deprives it of all strength, and, in no great length of time, reduces it to a mass of dry dust."* It identifies the turning point:

> "The alarm was greatly increased when, in 1810, the Queen Charlotte, a first-rate ship of war, shortly after launched at Deptford, was discovered, after a close examination, to have all her upper-works infected with the dry-rot; or, in other words, the ends of most of the beams, carlings, and ledges, the joinings of the planks, &c., were observed to be covered with a mouldy, fibrous, and reticulated crust, and the parts of the timber so covered to be perfectly rotten."

A brand-new first-rate warship, rotting before she saw action. The article notes that *"all the newspapers and journals of the day were filled with this alarming fact, and, in consequence thereof, a multitude of dry-rot doctors professed their assistance."*

### 3.2 Common Rot vs. Dry Rot

The 1823 article makes a crucial distinction that earlier editions lacked entirely. **Common rot** acts *externally inwards* — driven by weather exposure, it attacks wood "between wind and water." **Dry rot** acts *internally outwards* — caused by warm, moist, stagnant air, it destroys the internal fibres while the outer surface may appear sound:

> "Dry-rot is a disease in timber, occasioned by being shut up in warm, close, and moist situations, the effect of which is to destroy its fibres by a process acting internally outwards."

This distinction was not trivial. The Navy had been treating dry rot as if it were common rot — exposing ships on the stocks to "season" them in open air for years — which actually made things worse by alternately soaking and drying the timber.

### 3.3 The Disastrous Stove Experiment

One of the most vivid passages describes the Navy's attempt to cure the Queen Charlotte by heating:

> "Enormous fires were made in stoves placed in various parts of the ship, and the heat led in tubes to the cavities between the timbers, &c. The consequence of which was... an increase of the mischief they were intended to prevent. Every part of the ship was converted into a hot-house, and every part where the seeds of fungi had been deposited, began to throw out a luxuriant crop of mushrooms."

The attempted cure had literally created a fungal greenhouse.

### 3.4 Lukin's Fatal Experiment

Even more dramatic was the attempt by Mr Lukin at Woolwich Dock-yard to accelerate seasoning by replacing sap with turpentine vapour:

> "Before the process of transfusion was judged to be complete, an explosion took place, which proved fatal to six of the workmen, and wounded fourteen, two of whom shortly afterwards died. The explosion was like the shock of an earthquake; it demolished the wall of the dock-yard, part of which was thrown to the distance of 250 feet; an iron door, weighing 280 pounds, was driven to the distance of 230 feet."

The experiment was not repeated.

### 3.5 The Fungi Debate

The 1823 article captures a scientific debate still in progress. Mr Bowden of the Navy Office argued that dry rot *was* fungus — *"the nature of dry-rot is a vegetable substance"* — confusing cause and effect. Mr Wade had *"sounder notions"*: fungi were parasites that fed on already-decomposing wood, but also accelerated the decomposition. The article calls fungi *"the wolves and tigers of the vegetable world, destroying ultimately every plant they fix upon."*

By 1860, the [TIMBER](docs/1860/vol21.html#article-TIMBER) article states the matter plainly:

> "The decay of wood by the growth of fungae, denominated dry-rot, may be traced to the putrifying of the sap, when this has been left within the pores of the timber in the same condition as it exists in the living tree."

The science had settled: sap putrefaction was the cause; fungi were a consequence that accelerated decay.

## 4. Prevention and Preservation: An Evolving Science

### 4.1 Seasoning and Ventilation

Across all editions, the fundamental preventive is drying timber before use. But the methods evolved dramatically.

The 1823 DRY-ROT article debunks the myth that leaving ships on the stocks for years constituted "seasoning":

> "When a ship is built, exposed to the weather, the lower part forms a grand reservoir for all the rain that falls... Those timbers are always soaked with moisture... It cannot answer any end to have seven years wear out of a ship on the stocks."

By 1842, the [DOCK](docs/1842/vol8.html#article-DOCK) article reports that covered docks were finally being adopted — *"from the Swedes and the Venetians"* — to protect ships during construction.

By 1860, the TIMBER article makes a remarkable observation about "slop-built" merchant ships:

> "It is certainly unbecoming the scientific character of the age that ships built hurriedly and cheaply, and of very inferior timber, by what are contemptuously called 'slop' builders, are known to resist the ravages of dry-rot much better than the expensively and elaborately-constructed ships of Her Majesty's dockyards."

The reason? Cheap ships had spaces between their timbers that allowed air circulation. The Navy's tightly-packed construction created perfect conditions for rot. *"The dry-rot fungus will not thrive in an atmosphere less moist and stagnant than that of an underground cellar."*

### 4.2 Salt Water Immersion

The 1823 article tells the story of the Resistance, a frigate that sank in Malta harbour. Because she had already been condemned for dry rot, she was left underwater for months:

> "On her arrival in England it was observed that all appearance of fungus had vanished, and she remains a sound ship to this day."

This accidental discovery was then tested deliberately by sinking the Eden in Plymouth Sound for eighteen months. She was raised completely free of rot while her sister ship, the Mersey, *"was almost wholly covered with it."*

The French had known this all along — *"At Brest all the timber used in ship-building is deposited in the narrow creek of the harbour... and it is said that the Brest built ships never had the dry-rot."*

### 4.3 Chemical Preservation (1860)

By the 8th edition, the TIMBER article surveys the full range of chemical treatments that had been tried — and mostly failed:

- **Kyan's process** (chloride of mercury): Too expensive; *"the solution became so weak as, in a great measure, to lose its effect. This process has, therefore, been entirely abandoned."*
- **Margary's process** (sulphate of copper): *"Its efficacy, however, has proved doubtful."*
- **Burnett's process** (chloride of zinc): *"Still in use... but it cannot always be relied upon."*
- **Payne's process** (successive metallic and decomposing solutions): Theoretically promising but impractical.
- **Creosoting** (Bethell's patent, 1848): The most effective for outdoor wood — *"perfectly resists the attacks of marine worms and insects"* — but too flammable and smelly for ships or houses. Best for railway sleepers.

The article concludes that for ships, the answer remains *"summed up in two words, 'seasoning' and 'ventilation.'"*

## 5. The Oak Question

### 5.1 British Oak as National Resource

Oak occupies a unique place in these articles — it is simultaneously a botanical species, a strategic resource, and a symbol of national identity. The 1860 TIMBER article declares: *"As oak is by far the most valuable timber of English growth, the general inquiries we may enter into in the course of this article principally apply to it."*

The article distinguishes two native varieties:
- **Quercus pedunculata** (old English oak): acorns on stalks, leaves without stalks — *"almost all the English writers on timber have asserted"* its superiority
- **Quercus sessiliflora** (Durmast oak): acorns in clusters, leaves on stalks — *"the comparative inferiority of the timber"* is established, with 20-30 rings of sapwood vs. 12-15 for English oak

### 5.2 Soil Determines Quality

A recurring theme across editions is that the same species of oak varies enormously by soil:

> "The Sussex oak has always been celebrated as superior to all others. In France, the oak of Provence enjoys a similar reputation."

Marshy soil produces timber that is *"of a deep yellow, sometimes with a tinge of red... the texture is soft, and the fibre coarse"* — more prone to rot. Mountain oak (Scotland, Cumberland, Yorkshire) is *"proverbially hard and durable"* but stunted in size.

### 5.3 The Foreign Oak Question

By 1860, British oak could no longer supply the Navy, and the article surveys imports:

> "We obtain oak of excellent quality for planking from Poland and the shores of the Baltic; while from Italy, and from both sides of the Adriatic, sided timber and plank are imported in large quantities."

American oak was valued by cabinet-makers for its *"great size, uniform texture, straightness of grain, and little tendency to warp"* — but its specific gravity was *"somewhat less than that of English oak,"* making it second-class for shipbuilding.

## 6. Teak: The Superior Alternative

The most significant species comparison across the later editions is oak vs. teak. The 1860 TIMBER article gives teak a glowing assessment:

> "One of the most largely imported woods of tropical countries, and one of the most valuable, is teak. It grows to an enormous size, is particularly straight and free from knots, and has the peculiar property of resisting the attacks of insects. It possesses greater toughness than almost any other wood of equal weight, and is little liable to dry-rot or other disease."

The finest teak came from Malabar, followed by Travancore and Ceylon. Pegu and Moulmein teak was *"very inferior... comparatively coarse, porous, and open-grained"* — but this was attributed to swampy growing conditions, not inherent species weakness.

The Birmans tapped teak trees for oil used to protect their pagodas, and believed this weakened the timber. Despite this, their temples, *"built entirely of untap teak... although so fixed, the timber remains perfectly sound, notwithstanding many of them have stood nearly a century."*

Lloyd's classification of timbers placed in the **first class**: *"English oak, African oak, the live oak of America, the morra and green-heart of British Guiana, the teak and saul of India, and the iron bark of Australia."*

## 7. Pine and Mast Timber

The pine/fir articles reveal the global supply chain for naval masts — perhaps the most strategically critical timber product.

The 1860 TIMBER article distinguishes pines from firs: pines have *"less tapering"* trunks, more resinous wood, and are *"from shape, more adapted for masts."*

### 7.1 Riga Fir (Pinus Silvestris / Scots Pine)

> "The most valuable of all the varieties of fir timber is that which is called Riga Fir... which, although spread over a very large portion of the globe, appears to flourish in its greatest perfection in the forests of Lithuania and Poland."

It was *"not only extremely flexible and elastic, but is by far the most durable of all the pine timbers."* However, large sticks were becoming scarce: *"the use of it is confined to topsail-yards and the smaller description of spars."*

### 7.2 Yellow Pine (Pinus Strobus / White Pine)

> "Yellow Pine... which is imported from Canada, is the principal timber now available for large masts and yards."

Inferior to Riga fir in flexibility and durability, its *"great recommendations are its large size and its comparatively small cost."* But even this was becoming scarce: *"sticks of these large dimensions are becoming very scarce."*

### 7.3 American Timber Generally

The article is dismissive:

> "The timber of America in general is very inferior to that grown in the north of Europe, being much softer in its nature, not nearly so durable, and more liable to the dry-rot. American timber is therefore seldom used in ship-building, except for deck-deals."

## 8. The Changing Discourse: 1771-1860

### Phase 1: Unremarked Abundance (1771-1778)

In the 1st and 2nd editions, timber is barely discussed. TIMBER gets 8 words. WOOD is a brief botanical redirect. The 1771 [SHIP](docs/1771/vol3_S.html#article-SHIP) article discusses construction techniques without anxiety about materials. Timber is assumed to be available.

### Phase 2: Systematic Classification (1797-1810)

The 3rd and 4th editions see expansion of WOOD (4,601 words) and the first substantial SHIP-BUILDING articles. The [TREE](docs/1797/vol18.html#article-TREE) article (5,017 words) begins systematic botanical classification. But there is still no article on rot, and TIMBER remains a cross-reference.

### Phase 3: Crisis and Response (1815-1823)

The 5th and 6th editions capture the crisis moment. The 8,983-word DRY-ROT article appears in the 1823 supplement — the only edition to give it a dedicated entry. The DOCK article (17,610 words in 1842, building on 6th-edition content) devotes extensive space to covered docks as rot prevention. The discourse is dominated by the Navy's urgent practical needs.

### Phase 4: Scientific Consolidation (1842-1860)

By the 7th and 8th editions, the science has matured. The 1860 TIMBER article is a comprehensive treatise integrating:
- **Tree physiology** (sap flow, annual rings, bark stripping experiments by Knight, Buffon, Du Hamel)
- **Soil science** (detailed analysis of how soil type affects timber quality)
- **Global supply chains** (Baltic, Canadian, Indian, Australian timbers)
- **Preservation chemistry** (systematic evaluation of Kyan, Margary, Burnett, Payne, and creosoting)
- **The ventilation solution** (the surprising superiority of cheap "slop-built" ships)

The tone shifts from crisis to confident expertise. Dry rot is no longer mysterious — it is a solvable engineering problem.
