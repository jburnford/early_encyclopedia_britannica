# What the Britannica Got Right and Wrong: A Modern Mycological Assessment

*Comparing the Encyclopedia Britannica's understanding of wood decay, dry rot, and timber preservation (1771-1860) against current wood science and mycology.*

## 1. Is Dry Rot Really "Infectious"?

**The Britannica said (1823):** Dry rot is *"a most destructive, and apparently infectious, disease in timber"* that spreads from affected to sound wood via *"capillary attraction."*

**Modern science:** *Serpula lacrymans* (Wulfen) J.Schrot. — the true dry rot fungus — does indeed spread aggressively from infected to sound timber, but not via capillary attraction. It produces thick **rhizomorphs** (root-like hyphal strands, up to 2cm in diameter) that can traverse masonry, plaster, and brickwork for several metres to reach new wood. These rhizomorphs actively transport water *from* damp zones *to* dry wood, enabling the fungus to attack timber with moisture content that would normally be too low for fungal growth. This is a biological hydraulic mechanism, not capillary physics.

The fungus originated in high-altitude mountain forests of Northeast Asia (genomic studies, 2007-2018). Its natural habitat — dry, cold mountain forests with patchy, scattered wood — pre-adapted it to the built environment, where it must bridge gaps between timber elements through inert materials like masonry.

A single 1m-square fruiting body can release **3 billion spores per hour**. Concentrations of up to 360,000 spores/m^3 have been measured in contaminated basements.

**Verdict:** The Britannica's description of "infectious" spreading is **observationally correct**. The mechanism is wrong (rhizomorphs, not capillarity), but the practical implication — that infected timber must be removed and surrounding timber inspected — remains sound advice today.

## 2. Common Rot vs. Dry Rot — Mapping to Modern Categories

**The Britannica said (1823):** *"Common rot is a disease in timber, occasioned by the alternations of the weather acting on its surface, and destroying its fibres externally inwards. Dry-rot is a disease in timber, occasioned by being shut up in warm, close, and moist situations, the effect of which is to destroy its fibres by a process acting internally outwards."*

**Modern classification:**

| Category | Mechanism | Visual | Key Organisms |
|----------|-----------|--------|---------------|
| **Brown rot** | Degrades cellulose/hemicellulose via Fenton chemistry (oxalic acid + H2O2 + Fe2+ generate hydroxyl radicals); leaves lignin | Brown, cubical cracking, crumbly | *Serpula lacrymans*, *Postia placenta* |
| **White rot** | Degrades lignin AND cellulose using enzymes (laccases, peroxidases) | Pale, fibrous, spongy | *Trametes versicolor*, *Phanerochaete chrysosporium* |
| **Soft rot** | Degrades cellulose via cellulases; forms cavities in cell walls | Surface checking | Ascomycetes (*Chaetomium* spp.) |

The Britannica's "dry rot" maps well to **brown rot** by *S. lacrymans* — favoured by warm stagnant air and trapped moisture, producing the characteristic cubical cracking and brown discolouration described in the 1823 article. "Common rot" (external, weather-driven) encompasses **white rot** and **soft rot**, which predominate in exposed outdoor timber.

The modern term "dry rot" is itself somewhat misleading: the wood must be damp to decay. It only *appears* dry afterwards because the fungus consumes the water along with the cellulose.

**Verdict: PARTIALLY RIGHT.** The two-category system was a reasonable empirical classification. The Britannica missed the white/brown/soft rot distinction (which requires microscopy), but correctly identified the environmental conditions that favour each type.

## 3. The Sap Putrefaction Theory — The Big Error

**The Britannica said:** *"The real efficient cause then of the dry-rot, is that of the juices of the timber being brought into a state of putrefaction, occasioned generally by exposure to a moderate degree of heat and moisture in a stagnant atmosphere."* Fungi were secondary colonizers that accelerated an already-ongoing decomposition.

**Modern science:** This is **fundamentally backwards**. Robert Hartig, in his landmark 1878 publication *Die Zersetzungserscheinungen des Holzes*, was the first to prove experimentally that **fungi are the primary cause of wood decay**, not a secondary consequence of sap putrefaction. Before Hartig, observers assumed fungal filaments were a byproduct of decaying wood rather than its cause.

The causal chain is: fungal spores land on moist wood → spores germinate → hyphae penetrate cell walls → enzymatic breakdown of cellulose/lignin → structural collapse. Sap does not "putrefy" on its own in the absence of microorganisms.

**However**, the Britannica was right that **sap content matters**. Sapwood with high starch and sugar content is more susceptible to fungal colonisation because it provides readily available nutrients. The difference between sapwood and heartwood durability is largely explained by (a) heartwood extractives (toxic to fungi) and (b) sapwood nutrients (attractive to fungi). So the observation is correct; the causal arrow is reversed.

All Britannica editions (1797-1860) predate Hartig's paradigm shift. The 1860 article still states: *"The decay of wood by the growth of fungae, denominated dry-rot, may be traced to the putrifying of the sap."*

**Verdict: WRONG — but understandably so, and closer to right than it appears.** The emphasis on sap as a risk factor is correct; the claim that sap putrefies independently of fungi is not.

## 4. Fungal Reproduction by Spores — Ahead of Its Time

**The Britannica said (1823):** *"It is now, we apprehend, pretty well decided, that like other vegetables, the fungi or mushroom tribe are propagated by seed, so minute and numerous as to float about invisibly in the air."*

The article explicitly rejected the competing theory (adopted by Mr Bowden of the Navy Office and the *Quarterly Review*) that fungi arose by spontaneous generation from decomposing sap.

**Modern science:** Entirely confirmed. *S. lacrymans* reproduces by basidiospores (sexually) and by vegetative mycelial fragments. Spores remain viable for years and germinate when relative humidity exceeds 95% and wood moisture content exceeds ~30%.

**Verdict: REMARKABLY PRESCIENT.** The 1823 article's rejection of spontaneous generation in favour of spore-based reproduction was ahead of mainstream biology — the spontaneous generation debate was not settled until Pasteur's experiments in the **1860s**, four decades later. The 1797 FUNGI article had already affirmed Jussieu's position that *"Nature undoubtedly disseminates through the air, and over the surface of the earth, innumerable seeds of fungi."* The Britannica's mycologists were right before the bacteriologists.

## 5. Seasoning and Ventilation — Still Good Advice After 200 Years

**The Britannica said (1860):** *"The means serving our command for this purpose are summed up in the two words, 'seasoning' and 'ventilation.'"*

The article observed that cheap merchant ships with air gaps between timbers outlasted tightly-built Navy vessels: *"the dry-rot fungus will not thrive in an atmosphere less moist and stagnant than that of an underground cellar."*

**Modern science:** Wood-decay fungi require moisture content above **~25-28%** for initial colonisation, with optimal decay at 40-85% MC. The fibre saturation point (~28-30%) is the critical threshold. Below **20% moisture content**, decay essentially ceases. Kiln-dried lumber (12-19% MC) is effectively immune to fungal attack until re-wetted.

Ventilation removes moisture from timber surfaces, keeping MC below the fungal growth threshold. Modern building codes still mandate ventilation of timber structures, particularly crawl spaces, roof voids, and wall cavities — for exactly the reasons the Britannica identified.

**Verdict: CORRECT, and essentially unchanged after two centuries.** The practical advice — dry your timber, ventilate your structures — remains the foundation of wood preservation. The observation about "slop-built" ships is a perfect illustration of why air circulation matters more than timber quality.

## 6. Chemical Preservation — Remarkably Accurate Assessments

| Method | Britannica Assessment | Modern Assessment | Verdict |
|--------|----------------------|-------------------|---------|
| **Kyanizing** (HgCl2, Kyan 1832) | Too expensive; solution diluted to uselessness; *"entirely abandoned"* | Correct. Poor penetration, extremely toxic mercury compound. Abandoned worldwide. | **RIGHT** |
| **Burnett's** (ZnCl2, 1838) | *"Still in use... but cannot always be relied upon"* | Water-soluble, leaches out in wet conditions. Largely abandoned. | **RIGHT** |
| **Creosoting** (Bethell, 1838) | Most effective for railway sleepers; too flammable/smelly for ships or houses | **Still in industrial use** for railway sleepers and utility poles. EU phase-out extended to 2029. Full-cell pressure impregnation (Bethell's method) remains the gold standard technique. | **RIGHT** |
| **Salt water immersion** | Effective (the *Resistance* and *Eden* experiments) | Saltwater creates hostile conditions for most decay fungi. However, marine borers (Teredo shipworms) attack submerged wood — a problem the Britannica didn't address in these experiments. | **PARTIALLY RIGHT** |

Modern replacements include CCA (chromated copper arsenate, now restricted), ACQ (alkaline copper quaternary), copper azole, and micronised copper. All use **pressure impregnation** — the fundamental technique patented by Bethell in 1838. The Britannica correctly identified the most durable method and the most effective preservative of its era.

**Verdict: The Britannica's practical evaluations were LARGELY ACCURATE.** The hierarchy of effectiveness it identified (creosote > Burnett > Kyan) matches the historical record. Creosoting persisted for nearly 200 years before regulatory action began phasing it out on environmental grounds, not efficacy.

## 7. Winter Felling — The Britannica Got This One Wrong

**The Britannica said (1823):** The *Hawke* experiment debunked the ancient tradition that winter-felled timber was more durable. Half the sloop was built with winter-felled, bark-stripped timber; the other half with conventionally felled timber. After five years, *"no difference whatever could be discovered"* — both sides were rotten.

**Modern science:** There IS a measurable seasonal effect, though it is modest. **Sapwood starch content** varies seasonally: it peaks in spring/early summer (when the tree mobilises stored energy for new growth) and declines in autumn/winter. High starch content makes sapwood more attractive to fungi and insects (especially Lyctus beetles). Research on Scots pine confirms that starch rose at the beginning of the growth period and declined through winter.

The *Hawke* experiment likely failed to show a difference because both halves were exposed to the same poor ventilation conditions in the hull — the dominant factor overwhelming any modest benefit from felling season.

**Verdict: The Britannica's DEBUNKING was premature.** The folk wisdom had a real basis in sapwood starch cycling. The effect is small compared to seasoning, ventilation, and preservative treatment, but it is measurable and real. The *Hawke* was a poorly designed experiment — it tested felling season under conditions where ventilation was the controlling variable.

## 8. Bark Stripping Standing Trees — Partially Vindicated

**The Britannica reported:** Buffon (1738) and Du Hamel recommended stripping bark in spring and leaving the tree to die standing for 1-3 years before felling. Dr Hunter confirmed *"that by stripping off the bark, and allowing the tree to stand and die before it is cut, the sappy part becomes as hard and firm as the heart."*

**Modern science:** Girdling (ring-barking) **does** deplete starch from sapwood and reduce susceptibility to sapstain and mould fungi. Moisture content drops by more than 50% in sapwood of girdled conifers. However, there are serious downsides:
- Blue stain fungi colonise girdled trees within weeks
- Insect damage becomes likely (bark beetles are attracted to stressed trees)
- Incipient decay can develop under the remaining bark if the tree stands too long in moist conditions

Modern forestry does not recommend this practice for structural timber, though it persists in some tropical hardwood contexts.

**Verdict: PARTIALLY VINDICATED.** The underlying mechanism (starch depletion, moisture reduction) is real and measurable. The practice works best for species like teak that have strong natural extractive-based durability independent of starch content, which may explain why it persisted longest in tropical forestry.

## 9. Teak's "Oleaginous Matter" — Correct in Substance

**The Britannica said (1860):** *"The great durability of teak is to be attributed to the large amount of oleaginous matter contained in this timber."*

**Modern science:** Teak heartwood contains high concentrations of extractive compounds, dominated by **quinone derivatives** (35% of extractives), primarily **tectoquinone** (2-methylanthraquinone, ~28%). **Naphthoquinone** has been identified as the single most important compound for decay resistance. These extractives act as both:
- **Fungicides** — inhibiting hyphal growth and spore germination
- **Insecticides** — termicidal and anti-borer activity

Additionally, teak's natural oils create a water-repellent barrier, and its dense grain structure resists dimensional change. The Britannica also noted (via Leonard Wray) that teak from *"high, dry, and open land"* contained a *"mild oil"* while swampy teak contained *"an acid oil, which not only affects iron very materially."* This tracks with modern findings that plantation teak from short rotations on poor sites has lower tectoquinone content and correspondingly lower natural durability.

**Verdict: CORRECT in substance, imprecise in chemistry.** "Oleaginous matter" is a reasonable 19th-century description of tectoquinone and related naphthoquinone extractives dissolved in natural oils. The empirical observation — an oily chemical component, not just physical wood structure, confers rot resistance — was sound.

## 10. The Queen Charlotte Stove Disaster — A Perfect Natural Experiment

**The Britannica said (1823):** *"Enormous fires were made in stoves... the consequence of which was... Every part of the ship was converted into a hot-house, and every part where the seeds of fungi had been deposited, began to throw out a luxuriant crop of mushrooms."*

**Modern science:** This is **entirely consistent** with the biology of *Serpula lacrymans*:
- **Growth optimum:** 21-22C
- **Growth stops:** above 26-28C
- **Lethal temperature:** above 37-40C (sustained), or 50-70C within wood
- **Thermotolerance:** pre-exposure to 28C for 24 hours can create transient tolerance to otherwise lethal 40C

If the stoves raised temperatures into the 20-25C range (warm but not lethally hot), this would have provided **ideal growth conditions**. The heat would have driven moisture out of the wood, initially raising humidity in the enclosed hold — exactly the warm, humid, poorly ventilated environment that *S. lacrymans* thrives in. Fruiting body formation requires 30-40% MC in wood and high ambient humidity — conditions the stoves would have created perfectly.

To actually kill the fungus, temperatures would need to exceed 40C sustained throughout the entire hull structure — far beyond what stoves in a large ship hold could achieve or maintain uniformly.

**Verdict:** The Britannica recorded a **textbook illustration** of *S. lacrymans* temperature biology. The stove treatment failed because it heated the ship to the fungus's growth optimum rather than its lethal threshold. Modern remediation of dry rot in buildings uses **targeted hot-air treatment at 50-56C** — confirming that the principle (heat kills fungi) was right, but the execution (insufficiently hot, insufficiently uniform) was fatally wrong.

## The Bigger Picture

The Britannica's treatment of timber and dry rot across 90 years (1771-1860) presents a remarkable case study in pre-modern empirical science:

**What held up:** The practical advice — season your timber, ventilate your structures, use creosote for outdoor wood, choose teak or heartwood oak for durability. Two centuries of wood science have refined but not overturned these recommendations.

**What didn't:** The theoretical framework — sap putrefaction as primary cause, fungi as secondary. Robert Hartig's 1878 proof that fungi are the primary agents of wood decay inverted the causal model. This was part of the broader revolution in understanding biological causation (Koch's postulates, 1884; Pasteur's germ theory).

**What was surprisingly ahead of its time:** The 1823 article's correct identification of spore-based fungal reproduction, four decades before Pasteur settled the spontaneous generation debate. The Britannica's mycologists — drawing on Jussieu (1728) and the article's own "Mr Wade" — had the right answer before mainstream biology caught up.

The overall pattern is one familiar from the history of science: **empirical observation outpacing theoretical explanation**. The Britannica's writers could see what worked (ventilation, seasoning, creosote) and what didn't (stoves, Kyanizing), even when their theory of *why* was wrong. The practical knowledge of shipwrights, dockyard workers, and timber merchants — accumulated over centuries of trial and error — proved more durable than the scientific theories used to explain it.
